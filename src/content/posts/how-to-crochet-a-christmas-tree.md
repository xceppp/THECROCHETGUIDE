There are three completely different ways to crochet a Christmas tree, and picking the wrong one for what you actually want is the reason so many come out disappointing. A tree for a mantel needs to stand up. A tree for a garland needs to lie flat. They are not the same project.

This page covers all three, then the part they share: making a standing tree that does not topple the first time someone walks past the table.


![A finished green crochet Christmas tree standing on a wooden table](../../assets/articles/how-to-crochet-a-christmas-tree-result.jpg "Illustration. The finished look these steps build toward.")
## Which one do you want

| You want | Build it as |
| --- | --- |
| A tree that stands on a shelf | Tiered cone |
| A tree for a garland, card or applique | Flat triangle |
| A chunky tree with visible layers | Stacked rounds |

The tiered cone is the default and the one most people picture. Start there unless you know you want flat.

## What you need

- Worsted-weight yarn ([yarn weight chart](/yarn-weight-chart)) in green — or any color, a white tree looks better than it sounds
- A hook a size or two under the label for the stuffed versions, often a US G/6 (4.0 mm). See the [hook size chart](/crochet-hook-size-conversion-chart)
- Stuffing, and weight for the base: dry rice or pie weights in a small fabric bag
- Scraps for decoration, a yarn needle, a stitch marker

US abbreviations throughout. The [abbreviations chart](/crochet-abbreviations-chart) covers anything unfamiliar. Stitch counts here are a shape recipe, not a promise of finished inches — measure your own first tree and write the number down.

## Method 1: the tiered cone

![Hands crocheting a green cone in the round for a Christmas tree](../../assets/articles/how-to-crochet-a-christmas-tree-1.jpg "Illustration. Tiered cone — work in a spiral and mark the first stitch of every round.")

A plain cone reads as a party hat. What makes it read as a tree is the **tiers** — horizontal ridges that suggest layered branches. You get them from one trick: working stitches into the back loop only on a single round.

Work in a continuous spiral, marking the first stitch of each round.

**Rnd 1.** Magic ring, 6 sc. (6)

**Rnd 2.** 2 sc in each st around. (12)

**Rnd 3.** *Sc in next st, 2 sc in next st; repeat from * around. (18)

**Rnd 4.** *Sc in next 2 sts, 2 sc in next st; repeat from * around. (24)

**Rnd 5.** *Sc in next 3 sts, 2 sc in next st; repeat from * around. (30)

That is the base. Now build upward as a cone: work **two rounds even, then one increase round**, and repeat. Increasing on every third round rather than every round is what produces a slow taper instead of a bowl. The six-increases-per-round logic behind this is in [increases and decreases](/crochet-increase-decrease).

**The tier trick.** Every fourth or fifth round, work the entire round into the **back loop only**. The unused front loops stand out as a visible horizontal ridge all the way around. Three or four of these up the height of the tree is plenty; do it every round and you get a ribbed cone, not a tree.

Work upward until the opening is down to about six stitches, stuffing as you go, then *sc2tog* to close and fasten off.

<div class="tip">

Stuff in small amounts, continuously, as the cone narrows. It is nearly impossible to stuff the tip of a finished cone properly, and an unstuffed tip flops over — which is charming on a [gnome](/how-to-crochet-a-gnome) hat and just looks like a mistake on a tree.

</div>

## Method 2: the flat triangle

For garlands, cards, appliques and anything sewn onto something else.

Work bottom to top in rows, decreasing at both ends.

**Row 1.** Ch as many stitches as you want the base wide — 15 is a good size — then sc in the second ch from the hook and in each ch across.

**Row 2 and every row after.** Ch 1, turn, sc2tog, sc across to the last two stitches, sc2tog.

Repeat until three stitches remain, then sc3tog and fasten off. The edges come out cleaner if you keep the turning chain consistent; if your triangles are coming out with wavy sides, that is usually a turning chain problem rather than a decreasing problem, and [the turning chain](/crochet-turning-chain) explains it.

Add a two-stitch-wide brown rectangle at the bottom for a trunk if the tree is going on a card. Skip it for a garland, where the hanging thread reads as the trunk anyway.

## Method 3: stacked rounds

The chunkiest and the fastest. Make three flat circles in decreasing sizes — the first five rounds above give you the largest; stop at round 4 and round 3 for the others — then fold each into a shallow cone by overlapping the edges slightly and stitching them closed.

Stack the three cones on a length of dowel, a chopstick, or a tight tube of brown crochet, largest at the bottom. The gaps between layers are the point of this version.

This one is genuinely faster than the tiered cone and looks more deliberate at a larger size. It is the version to make if you want something substantial for a mantel in an evening.

## Making it stand up

![A green crochet Christmas tree being stuffed](../../assets/articles/how-to-crochet-a-christmas-tree-2.jpg "Illustration. Stuff firmly and weight the base so the tree stands.")

A tree is a cone, which means it is inherently top-heavy the moment it is more than a few inches tall. Three things fix it, and you usually want all three.

**Weight the base.** Dry rice or pie weights in a small fabric bag, sitting flat on the bottom before you add any stuffing. Use a bag rather than loose rice, or the grains migrate out through the stitches over a couple of seasons.

**Keep the base genuinely flat.** If the first five rounds ruffled or domed at all, the tree rocks. Press the base flat on a table and check it before you start stuffing — once stuffed, the shape is set.

**Do not over-stuff the upper half.** Firm at the bottom, lighter toward the tip. Packing the top hard raises the center of gravity for no benefit.

## Decorating without ruining it

![Sewing a brown trunk onto a crochet Christmas tree](../../assets/articles/how-to-crochet-a-christmas-tree-3.jpg "Illustration. Add the trunk and light decorations after the shape is stable.")

**French knots** in contrasting yarn are the tidiest ornaments and the fastest.

**Small beads or buttons** look sharp and are a choking hazard on anything going to a small child. Embroidered dots instead for a child's gift.

**A star on top** works better sewn flat against the tip than standing upright, unless you stiffen it — see the blocking method in [how to crochet a snowflake](/how-to-crochet-a-snowflake), which is the same problem.

**Less than you think.** A tree with six ornaments looks decorated. A tree with thirty looks like a textured green blob.

## Mistakes that spoil the tree

**Increasing every round.** The commonest error by far, and it produces a bowl. The cone comes from spacing increases out across rounds worked even.

**Back-loop tiers on every round.** That is a ribbed cone. Three or four ridges total.

**No weight in the base.** It will stand while you are looking at it.

**Stuffing the tip at the end.** Cannot be done properly. Stuff continuously.

**Stacking increases in a vertical line.** Working increases directly above each other on every increase round creates a seam that pulls the cone crooked. Offset them by a stitch or two.

## Frequently asked

### Why is my cone leaning?

Either the weight is sitting off-center, or the increases lined up into a seam. Roll the finished tree on a table — if it always settles the same way, the weight is off-center and you can usually reposition it through the bottom opening before closing.

### Can I make a tree without stuffing it?

Yes, over a cone-shaped craft foam form. It is faster, it stands perfectly, and it costs more than the yarn does. Worth it if you are making a matched set.

### What is the back loop only doing, exactly?

Each stitch has two loops at the top. Working into only the back one leaves the front loop unworked, and that unused loop stands proud of the surface as a ridge. It is the same technique used for ribbing elsewhere in crochet, just applied to one round at a time.

### How do I get a whole set the same size?

Work from stitch counts and keep the same hook and yarn for all of them, then count rounds rather than measuring. Two trees with identical round counts will match; two measured to the same inch mark usually will not, because stuffing density varies.

### What should I make next?

For a set that fills a mantel, pair trees with [snowflakes](/how-to-crochet-a-snowflake) for contrast between flat and stuffed, or add a [Santa](/crochet-santa-amigurumi-for-beginners) as the centerpiece. If you are choosing yarn for a whole seasonal batch, [yarn for Christmas crochet](/yarn-for-christmas-crochet) covers what works and what fights you.
