There are more free crochet patterns than anyone could work through in a lifetime. The hard part is telling a well-written pattern from a bad one, and spotting the ones that were never real patterns at all.

**Every link on this page goes to the designer or publisher directly.** Patterns belong to the people who wrote them. Nothing here reproduces their instructions.

## Where to look

![Laptop open beside a printed crochet pattern yarn and hook](../../assets/articles/where-to-find-free-crochet-patterns-2.jpg "Illustration. Start with known libraries, then verify the pattern page still opens.")

**[Ravelry](https://www.ravelry.com/patterns/search#craft=crochet&availability=free)** is the first place to look. It indexes patterns from designers, publishers, and yarn companies, and you can filter by free, by craft, by yarn weight, by hook size, and by category. A free account is required to use the filters.

Start there for the **project pages**. Every pattern shows finished versions made by other people, with their notes. Before you commit a weekend, you can see whether other crocheters found the instructions wrong.

**[LoveCrafts](https://www.lovecrafts.com/en-us/l/crochet/crochet-patterns)** carries a large mixed library of free and paid patterns from independent designers, with a filter for free.

Yarn companies publish patterns free because they want you buying their yarn. The patterns are edited, tested, and available as printable PDFs. For beginners, this is often the best free material available.

**[Red Heart](https://www.redheart.com/free-patterns)** and **[Yarnspirations](https://www.yarnspirations.com/collections/patterns)** — Yarnspirations is the shared home for Red Heart, Bernat, Caron, and Patons, so one search covers several of the biggest American yarn brands. Strong on blankets, beginner accessories, and baby items, with clear skill-level labels.

**[Lion Brand](https://www.lionbrand.com/)** publishes a similarly large free library, well written and beginner-friendly, searchable from their patterns section.

**DROPS Design**, a Norwegian company, has one of the largest genuinely free pattern libraries, including a lot of garments. Search for it by name. The patterns are translated from Norwegian and use European conventions, so read the abbreviations before you start. The [US and UK terminology differences](/crochet-abbreviations-chart) apply to translated patterns too.

Individual designers publish free patterns on their own sites, with a paid PDF as an option. Quality varies widely. If one designer's explanations suit you, the rest of their catalog probably will too.

**[Moogly](https://www.mooglyblog.com/free-crochet-patterns/)** is a reliable example: a long-running catalog, clearly written, with photo tutorials and consistent formatting.

Free patterns are enough to crochet for years. Paying is worth it for complex garments that have been graded across many sizes and technically edited, and for supporting a designer whose work you use.

## What to check before you buy yarn

![Notebook project list beside yarn and a hook](../../assets/articles/where-to-find-free-crochet-patterns-1.jpg "Illustration. Clear materials, gauge, and photos beat a pretty cover shot alone.")

A pattern missing several of these is going to waste your time.

- **A gauge statement.** On a garment, its absence means the designer did not think about size.
- **Total yardage**, not just "3 skeins." Skein sizes vary between brands.
- **Stitch counts at the end of rows**, written as `(24 sts)`. These are your checkpoints. Without them you cannot catch a mistake before row forty.
- **A stated terminology**, US or UK. If it is not stated, look for `sc` (US) or `htr` (UK).
- **A special stitches section** if the pattern uses bobble, cluster, or shell. Those words mean different things to different designers.
- **Finished measurements**, so you know whether it fits before you start.
- **More than one photo**, ideally from more than one angle.

<div class="tip">

**The best check takes thirty seconds.** Search the pattern on Ravelry and read other people's project notes. If three separate people say the crown decreases are wrong, they are wrong, and the comments usually contain the fix.

</div>

## Common mistakes

Pinterest and Facebook are full of AI-generated images of crochet that was never made and cannot be made. The link goes to no pattern, or to instructions that produce nothing.

**Look at the stitches.** Real crochet has a regular, repeating structure. In an AI image, stitches dissolve, change direction for no reason, or merge into the background.

**Be suspicious of impossible perfection.** Flawlessly even gradients, geometry too precise for handwork, colors that no yarn manufacturer sells together, or lighting that looks like a render.

**Check the hands, the scale, and the byline.** Fingers go wrong, and the item's size does not match whatever it sits on. A real pattern names an author, a gauge, a yarn, a hook size, and a yardage. A fake one has an image and a vibe.

**Check Ravelry.** A pattern spread across a social feed will have project pages. No projects at all is a strong signal. Start from the sources above. Those were made by somebody with actual hands.

## Frequently asked

**Can I sell what I make from a free pattern?** Usually yes, but it depends on the designer, and the answer is on their pattern page. Yarn company patterns generally permit it. Selling the pattern itself, or copying its text, is never allowed.

**What is a good first project?** Something flat, one color, one stitch, in worsted acrylic. A dishcloth or a simple scarf. Practice reading instructions and keeping a count before you also take on shaping, color changes, and gauge.

**A pattern seems mathematically impossible. Is it me?** Not always. Search the designer's name plus "errata," and check the Ravelry comments. A wrong stitch count is often a known, documented mistake.
