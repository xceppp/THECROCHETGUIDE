There are more free crochet patterns available than anyone could work through in a lifetime. The problem is not finding patterns — it is that a fair number of them are badly written, and a growing number are not real patterns at all.

Here is where to look, and how to judge what you find.

**Every link on this page goes to the designer or publisher directly.** No pattern instructions are reproduced here. Patterns belong to the people who wrote them, and the correct thing to do is send you to their page.

## The big pattern databases

**[Ravelry](https://www.ravelry.com/patterns/search#craft=crochet&availability=free)** is the serious crocheter's search engine and the first place to look. It indexes hundreds of thousands of patterns from designers, publishers, and yarn companies, and you can filter by free, by craft, by yarn weight, by hook size, and by category all at once.

The reason to start here is not the size of the database — it is the **project pages**. Every pattern shows you finished versions made by other people, with their notes and complaints. Before you commit a weekend to something, you can see whether twenty other crocheters found the sleeve instructions wrong. Nothing else in crochet gives you that. A free account is required to use the filters.

**[LoveCrafts](https://www.lovecrafts.com/en-us/l/crochet/crochet-patterns)** carries a large mixed library of free and paid patterns from independent designers, with a filter for free.

## Yarn company libraries

Yarn companies publish patterns free because they want you buying their yarn. This works out well for you: the patterns are professionally written, technically edited, tested before publication, and available as clean printable PDFs. For beginners this is often the highest-quality free material available anywhere.

**[Red Heart](https://www.redheart.com/free-patterns)** and **[Yarnspirations](https://www.yarnspirations.com/collections/patterns)** — Yarnspirations is the shared home for Red Heart, Bernat, Caron, and Patons, so one search covers several of the biggest American yarn brands. Strong on blankets, beginner accessories, and baby items, with clear skill-level labels.

**[Lion Brand](https://www.lionbrand.com/)** publishes a similarly large free library, well written and beginner-friendly, searchable from their patterns section.

**DROPS Design**, a Norwegian company, has one of the largest genuinely free pattern libraries in existence, including a lot of garments. Worth searching for by name. Be aware their patterns are translated from Norwegian and use European conventions, so read the abbreviations section carefully before starting — the [US and UK terminology differences](/crochet-abbreviations-chart) apply to translated patterns too.

## Independent designers and blogs

Individual designers publish free patterns on their own sites, usually supported by ads, with a paid ad-free PDF as an option. The quality range is enormous — the best independent patterns are better than anything a yarn company publishes, and the worst are unusable.

**[Moogly](https://www.mooglyblog.com/free-crochet-patterns/)** is a good example of the reliable end: a long-running catalog of free patterns, clearly written, with photo tutorials and consistent formatting.

The general rule with independent designers is to judge the individual designer rather than the category. Once you find one whose instructions make sense to you, work through their whole catalog — a designer's style is consistent, and if their explanations suit your brain, all of their patterns will.

## How to tell a good free pattern from a bad one

Check for these before you buy yarn. A pattern missing several is going to waste your time.

- **A gauge statement.** Its absence means the designer did not think about size. On a garment, that is disqualifying.
- **Total yardage**, not just "3 skeins." Skein sizes vary wildly between brands, so a skein count alone tells you nothing.
- **Stitch counts at the end of rows**, written as `(24 sts)`. These are your error checkpoints. A pattern without them gives you no way to catch a mistake before row forty.
- **A stated terminology**, US or UK. If it is not stated, look for `sc` (US) or `htr` (UK) to work it out.
- **A special stitches section** if the pattern uses anything unusual. Words like bobble, cluster, and shell mean different things to different designers, so a good pattern defines its own.
- **Finished measurements.** So you know whether it fits before you start.
- **More than one photo**, ideally from more than one angle.

**The best single check takes thirty seconds:** search the pattern on Ravelry and read other people's project notes. If three separate people say the crown decreases are wrong, they are wrong, and the comments usually contain the fix.

## The fake patterns problem

This is new, it is getting worse, and it will cost you an afternoon if you do not know about it.

Pinterest and Facebook are now full of AI-generated images of crochet items that were never made and cannot be made. The picture shows something impossible — stitches that do not connect, structures no hook could produce, a texture that is not crochet at all — and the link goes to a page with either no pattern or an invented set of instructions that produces nothing.

How to spot them:

**Look at the stitches, not the object.** Real crochet has a visible, regular, repeating structure. AI images have areas where the stitches dissolve into mush, change direction for no reason, or merge into the background.

**Be suspicious of impossible perfection.** Flawlessly even gradients, geometry too precise for handwork, colors that no yarn manufacturer sells together, or lighting that looks like a render.

**Check the hands and the scale.** If a person is holding it, look closely at the fingers. Look at whether the item's size makes sense against whatever it sits on.

**Look for a designer.** Real patterns have a named author, a gauge, a yarn, a hook size, and a yardage. Fake ones have an image and a vibe.

**Check Ravelry.** If a pattern is genuinely popular enough to be all over Pinterest, it will have project pages. No projects at all is a strong signal.

The reliable defense is simply to start from the sources listed above rather than from a social feed. Everything on Ravelry, on a yarn company's site, or on an established designer's blog was made by somebody with actual hands.

## Free versus paid

Free patterns are genuinely enough. You could crochet for years without paying for one, and the yarn company libraries alone would keep you busy indefinitely.

Paying is worth it in two situations. **Complex garments**, where you want a pattern that has been graded across many sizes and technically edited — that work is expensive to do properly and it shows. And **supporting a designer whose work you use**, which is the reason most of the free patterns you enjoy continue to exist.

## Frequently asked

**Can I sell what I make from a free pattern?** Usually yes, but it depends on the designer, and the answer is on their pattern page. Yarn company patterns generally permit it. Selling *the pattern itself*, or copying its text anywhere, is never allowed regardless of how the finished item is treated.

**Why do free patterns have so many ads?** Because that is what pays for them. A designer spends days writing, testing, and photographing a pattern and gives it away; the ads on the page are the payment. Most also sell an ad-free PDF for a couple of dollars if the ads bother you.

**What is a good first project?** Something flat, one color, one stitch, in worsted acrylic. A dishcloth or a simple scarf. You want to practice reading instructions and keeping a consistent count without also fighting shaping, color changes, and gauge at once.

**A pattern seems mathematically impossible. Is it me?** Not always. Search the designer's name plus "errata," and check the Ravelry comments. Published patterns do contain errors, and a wrong stitch count is often a known and documented mistake.
