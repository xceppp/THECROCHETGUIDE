Crochet patterns are written in a compressed shorthand that every designer assumes you already read fluently. Nobody sits you down and explains it. You are just handed something like this:

> **Row 4:** Ch 3 (counts as dc), *2 dc in next st, sk 1 st, rep from * across, turn. (24 sts)

By the end of this page you will read that line without stopping. We will go through a pattern in the order you actually meet it, from top to bottom.

## Start at the bottom, not the top

The first useful move is counterintuitive: **read the whole pattern before you pick up your hook.** Not to memorize it — to check for three things.

1. **A "special stitches" section.** If the designer invented a stitch or uses an unusual one, they define it near the top. Miss that section and nothing will make sense on row six.
2. **Whether it is US or UK terms.** This determines what every abbreviation means. Details below.
3. **Whether the construction makes sense to you.** Is it worked flat and seamed, or in one piece in the round? Is the hat made brim-first or crown-first? Knowing the shape of the journey prevents most panic.

Five minutes here saves hours later.

## The materials list

**Yarn.** A good pattern gives you three things: the weight (a number 0 to 7, or a name like worsted or DK), the total yardage, and the specific yarn the designer used. Yardage is the number that matters — buy by yards or meters, not by number of balls, because ball sizes differ wildly between brands. Add ten percent to be safe.

**Hook.** Usually given as a letter and a millimeter measurement. Trust the millimeters; the letters are inconsistent between manufacturers. Our [hook size chart](/crochet-hook-size-conversion-chart) covers every conversion.

**Gauge.** Something like `16 sc and 18 rows = 4 inches`. This is the single most ignored line in crochet and the reason things come out the wrong size.

**Notions.** Stitch markers, a tapestry needle, safety eyes, buttons. Check this before you start rather than at 11pm mid-project.

**Finished size.** Compare this to the person it is for. If a hat is listed at 21 inches circumference and you need 23, you know now instead of after.

## Gauge, and whether you can skip it

Gauge is how many stitches and rows fit into a set measurement, almost always 4 inches, worked in the stitch the pattern uses.

It exists because your hands are not the designer's hands. Two people can follow identical instructions with identical yarn and identical hooks and produce items an inch or two different in size, because one of them holds the yarn tighter.

To check it: work a swatch a bit larger than 4 inches square in the pattern's stitch, lay it flat without stretching, and count the stitches across the middle 4 inches.

- **Too many stitches in 4 inches** means your work is tight. Go **up** a hook size.
- **Too few stitches** means your work is loose. Go **down** a hook size.

<div class="tip">

**When you can honestly skip it:** blankets, scarves, dishcloths, coasters, bags — anything where being slightly bigger or smaller does not matter.

**When skipping it will cost you:** anything worn. Hats, sweaters, mittens, socks, and gloves. Also amigurumi, where loose gauge lets the stuffing show through the fabric.

</div>

## Which dialect is it written in?

There are two sets of stitch names, and they use the same abbreviations for different stitches. US terms and UK terms are shifted by one: a US single crochet is a UK double crochet.

The pattern often will not tell you. Two quick tests:

- **If you see `sc` anywhere, it is US terms.** UK terminology does not have a single crochet at all.
- **If you see `htr` (half treble), it is UK terms.** That abbreviation does not exist in US patterns.

The full conversion is in the [abbreviations chart](/crochet-abbreviations-chart). When you genuinely cannot tell, assume US — that is what most English-language patterns online use.

## Rows versus rounds

**Rows** mean flat work. You reach the end, turn your work over, and come back the other way. A pattern will say `turn` at the end of each row, and there will be a turning chain at the start of the next.

**Rounds** mean circular work, and they split into two kinds:

- **Joined rounds** — you finish the round, join to the first stitch with a slip stitch, and chain up to start the next. This leaves a visible seam line, which is fine and sometimes intentional.
- **Spiral rounds** — you just keep going without joining, common in amigurumi. There is no seam, but there is also nothing marking where a round starts, which is why patterns tell you to place a stitch marker in the first stitch and move it up each round. Do it. Counting a spiral without a marker is miserable.

## Reading a single line

Now the shorthand itself. Here is that line again:

> **Row 4:** Ch 3 (counts as dc), *2 dc in next st, sk 1 st, rep from * across, turn. (24 sts)

Broken into pieces:

- **`Row 4:`** — which row you are on. If it says `Rows 4–12:` the same instruction repeats for all of them.
- **`Ch 3 (counts as dc)`** — chain 3 to gain height, and treat that chain as the row's first double crochet. This matters for counting. If it said "does not count as a stitch," you would work into the very first stitch as well.
- **`*`** — everything from here to `rep from *` is the repeat.
- **`2 dc in next st`** — two double crochets into a single stitch. That is an increase.
- **`sk 1 st`** — skip the next stitch entirely.
- **`rep from * across`** — keep alternating those two instructions until you reach the end of the row.
- **`turn`** — flip the work to start the next row.
- **`(24 sts)`** — you should have 24 stitches when you finish. This is a checkpoint, not an instruction.

### The punctuation rules

- **`( )` parentheses** hold a group worked into the *same place*: `(2 dc, ch 1, 2 dc) in next st` all goes into one stitch. But parentheses at the end of a row give the stitch count instead: `(24 sts)`. Context tells you which.
- **`[ ]` brackets** hold a group repeated a set number of times: `[sc, ch 1] x 6` means do that pair six times.
- **`*` asterisks** mark a repeat that runs to the end of the row or round.
- **A number before a stitch** is how many to make: `3 dc`.
- **A number inside the stitch name** is part of one combined stitch: `dc3tog` is a single decrease made out of three stitches.

## Count your stitches

The number in parentheses at the end of each row exists so you can catch a mistake immediately instead of thirty rows later. Use it.

Almost all stitch count drift comes from three causes:

**The turning chain.** Designers disagree on whether it counts as a stitch. Whichever your pattern says, be consistent — inconsistency is what makes edges slant.

**The last stitch of the row.** It is easy to miss the final stitch, especially in double crochet where the turning chain from the previous row looks like part of the fabric. If you lose exactly one stitch per row, this is it.

**Working into the chain instead of the space.** When a pattern says `ch-2 sp`, put the hook through the *hole*, not into the individual chains. Doing it wrong changes both your count and your gauge.

If your count is off by one or two, undo back to the last row where you were certain. It is faster than compensating, and compensating never actually works.

## Sizes written inside one pattern

Garment patterns cover several sizes at once, like this:

> Ch 60 (66, 72, 78)

The first number is the smallest size and the rest follow in order, matching the size list at the top of the pattern.

Before row one, go through the whole pattern and **circle every number for your size**. Every experienced crocheter does this and every beginner learns to after getting it wrong once.

## Pattern repeats and multiples

You will see things like `worked in multiples of 6 + 2`. That tells you how to change the size yourself: your starting chain has to be a multiple of six, plus two extra. So 20, 26, 32, and 38 all work. 25 does not, and the pattern will break at the end of the first row.

## The parts nobody reads that you should

**"Notes."** Designers put the genuinely important warnings here — that the pattern runs small, that the first round is fiddly, that the yarn splits. It is the most useful paragraph in most patterns and the most skipped.

**Stitch diagrams.** Common in Japanese patterns and lace, where the shape is easier to see than to describe. A separate visual language, well worth learning if you like lace.

**Pattern errata.** If a published pattern seems mathematically impossible, search the designer's name plus "errata." Patterns do get published with mistakes, and it is often not you.

## Frequently asked

**Do I need to know every abbreviation before starting?** No. Keep the [abbreviations chart](/crochet-abbreviations-chart) open and look things up as you go. You will absorb the common fifteen within a month without trying.

**Why does my work keep getting narrower or wider?** Stitch count drift, almost certainly from the turning chain or the last stitch of the row. Count every row for a while and it will surface quickly.

**What is a good first pattern?** Something flat, in one color, in worsted yarn, worked in rows of a single stitch. A dishcloth or a simple scarf. You want to practice reading instructions without also fighting shaping, color changes, and gauge at the same time.
