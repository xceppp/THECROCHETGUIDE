The magic ring — also called the magic circle or adjustable ring — is how you start crocheting in the round when you need the center to close completely.

It is the standard start for amigurumi, hats worked crown-first, granny squares without a center hole, and circular motifs. It is also the technique beginners abandon most often, usually for one specific reason covered below.

## Why not just chain a ring?

The old method is to chain four or five, then join with a slip stitch to make a ring, and work your first round into that ring.

This works, and for many projects it is completely fine. But the ring is a fixed size, so it leaves a small permanent hole in the middle. On a doily that hole is a design feature. On a stuffed toy, it is a window that shows the stuffing.

The magic ring solves that: you work into an adjustable loop, then pull the tail and the center cinches shut like a drawstring.

## The steps

You need a hook and yarn. Nothing else.

**1. Make the loop.** Lay the yarn across your palm with the tail hanging down about six inches. Wrap the working yarn around two fingers to form a circle, bringing it back across so the working strand crosses **over** the tail. You should now have a ring made of two strands where they overlap.

**2. Insert the hook.** Slide the hook into the middle of the ring, under both strands, and catch the working yarn.

**3. Pull up a loop.** Draw the working yarn back through the ring. One loop on the hook.

**4. Chain one to secure it.** Yarn over and pull through that loop. This chain anchors the ring and does not count as a stitch.

**5. Work your first round into the ring.** Here is the part that matters: each stitch goes **over both strands of the ring** — the loop strand and the tail together — not into a stitch. You are wrapping your stitches around the doubled ring like beads on a string.

For amigurumi this is usually 6 single crochets. Other patterns will specify their own number.

**6. Cinch it closed.** When the round is complete, pull the loose tail firmly. The center draws shut. Give it a proper tug — it should disappear entirely.

**7. Join or continue.** For joined rounds, slip stitch into the first stitch of the round. For a spiral, which is standard in amigurumi, just keep going and put a stitch marker in the first stitch so you know where the round began.

## The mistake that makes people give up

**If your ring will not close, you worked into only one strand.**

This is the single most common failure, it is almost always the cause, and it is invisible until you pull the tail and nothing happens. When your hook goes into the ring, it has to pass under **both** overlapping strands so your stitches encircle the whole bundle. Catch only the working strand and the tail is not held by anything, so pulling it does nothing.

Undo it and start again. There is no way to fix it after the fact.

<div class="warning">

**Do not pull the tail until the round is finished.** Cinching early locks the ring at a size you cannot work into, and you will have to undo the whole round. Stitches first, tail second, always.

</div>

## The other things that go wrong

**It loosens again later.** Weave the tail through the back of a few stitches on the underside before you stuff anything. An un-woven tail will work itself loose with handling, and a toy's center opening back up after assembly is genuinely annoying to reach.

**It looks like a hexagon, not a circle.** Six single crochets in the first round is a small number and it can look angular at first. It rounds out over the next two or three rounds as the stitch count grows. Keep going.

**You lose track of where the round starts.** In a spiral there is nothing marking it. Use a stitch marker — a scrap of contrasting yarn works — in the first stitch of each round, and move it up as you pass. This is not optional advice; counting a spiral without a marker is how projects get abandoned.

**Your center is too tight to work into.** Loosen the initial ring slightly before step 2. It should be about the size of a dime, not pulled snug.

## What to work into the ring

The magic ring accepts any stitch. The count depends on the stitch height, because taller stitches take up more room around the ring.

| First round | Typical count | Common use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| single crochet | 6 | Amigurumi, toys |
| half double crochet | 8 | Denser hats, small motifs |
| double crochet | 12 | Granny squares, hats, circular motifs |
| treble crochet | 16 | Open lace motifs |

Your pattern will give you its own number. Trust the pattern over this table — designers choose the first round to make their increase math work.

## Keeping a flat circle flat

If you continue in the round after the magic ring and your circle either cups upward like a bowl or ruffles like a lettuce leaf, the increase rate is wrong.

A flat circle in single crochet needs the same number of increases in every round as you had stitches in the first round. Start with 6, and every round after adds 6: round one has 6 stitches, round two has 12, round three has 18, round four has 24, and so on.

- **Cupping upward** means too few increases. This is what you *want* for a hat or a toy, deliberately — it is how the shape turns into a dome.
- **Ruffling and waving** means too many increases. The circle has more fabric than it needs to lie flat.

## Frequently asked

**Is the magic ring the same as the magic circle?** Yes. Same technique, different names, along with "adjustable ring." Patterns use all three.

**Can I use it for hats?** Yes, if the hat is worked crown-first from the top down. Hats worked as a flat strip and seamed, or brim-up, do not need it.

**Do I have to use it?** No. The chain-ring method works for anything where a small center hole does not matter — doilies, open motifs, most granny squares. Use the magic ring when the center must be closed.

**Why does my first round feel awkward and floppy?** Because you are working into a loop instead of into stitches, so there is nothing firm to hold. It feels wrong to everyone the first several times. It becomes automatic quickly, and the fabric does not care.

**How many stitches should I make in the ring?** Whatever the pattern says. If you are improvising, 6 single crochets is the standard amigurumi start and 12 double crochets is the standard for a flat circular motif.
