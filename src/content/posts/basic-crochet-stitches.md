Almost everything in crochet is five stitches. Shells, bobbles, clusters, and puffs are these five combined. Learn them shortest to tallest. Names are **US terms**.

Use medium worsted in a light solid color and a 5 mm (H/8) hook. Dark yarn and variegated yarn hide the structure you need to see.


![A cream crochet swatch showing basic stitches beside a hook](../../assets/articles/basic-crochet-stitches-result.jpg "Illustration. Five stitches build almost everything — learn them in height order.")
## The two moves

**Yarn over (`yo`)** — wrap the working yarn over the hook from back to front.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/yarn-over.svg" alt="Diagram of a yarn over. The working yarn wraps over the hook from back to front." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Yarn over moves from the back of the hook toward the front. It is not a photograph.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Pull through** — draw the hook, with yarn on it, back through a loop or a stitch.

The five stitches differ only in how many times you yarn over first, and how many loops you close at a time.

## The foundation chain

![Hands making a foundation chain in cream yarn](../../assets/articles/basic-crochet-stitches-1.jpg "Illustration. The foundation chain is where every flat piece starts.")

Every flat piece starts with a chain. The chain starts with a slip knot, which is not a stitch and does not count.

**Slip knot**

1. Cross the tail over the working yarn so you have a loop, with the tail hanging down.
2. Reach through that loop and pull a new loop of working yarn through it.
3. Put the new loop on the hook.
4. Pull the tail and the working yarn in opposite directions until the knot sits against the hook.

The knot should slide. If it will not move, the yarn hand is too tight. Loosen it before you chain. The loop on the hook is not a chain.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/slip-knot.svg" alt="Diagram of a slip knot in three steps: cross the tail, pull a loop through, and seat it on the hook so it still slides." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. The slip knot is adjustable. The loop on the hook is not a chain.</figcaption>
</figure>

Then chain:

1. Yarn over.
2. Pull that yarn through the loop on the hook. That is one chain.
3. Repeat for the number the pattern asks for.

Do not count the slip knot or the loop on the hook. Only the finished V-shaped links count. Chain about 20, and keep it loose.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/foundation-chain.svg" alt="Diagram of a foundation chain. The front of each chain is a V, the back is a bump, and the loop on the hook is not counted." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Count the finished V links. Skip the slip knot and the loop still on the hook.</figcaption>
</figure>

<div class="tip">

The front of a chain is a row of Vs. The back has a raised bump. Work under one strand of the V, under both, or into the bump. All three are correct. The bump gives the neatest edge. Do the same thing all the way across.

</div>

## 1. Slip stitch (`sl st`)

The shortest stitch. It barely adds height. Use it to join rounds, move the hook sideways, or seam.

1. Insert the hook into the stitch.
2. Yarn over.
3. Pull through the stitch and the loop on the hook in one movement.

One loop remains.

## 2. Single crochet (`sc`)

![Close-up of single crochet stitches being formed](../../assets/articles/basic-crochet-stitches-2.jpg "Illustration. Single crochet — short, dense, and the stitch most beginners live in.")

Short, dense, and sturdy. Use it for amigurumi, bags, and dishcloths.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/insert-single-crochet.svg" alt="Diagram of the top of a stitch, with a front loop and a back loop, and the hook going under both." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Ordinary single crochet goes under both loops at the top of the stitch.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/single-crochet.svg" alt="Diagram of single crochet in three steps: insert under both loops, pull up a loop so two loops are on the hook, then pull through both." width="640" height="430" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Single crochet. Two loops on the hook, then yarn over and pull through both.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Insert the hook into the next stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop. Two loops on the hook.
3. Yarn over and pull through both loops.

Into a foundation chain, start in the **second chain from the hook**.

## Practice: a 15-stitch rectangle

This is a counting exercise, not a finished object. No sample of it was measured for this page.

Chain 16. Count the finished V links only. The loop on the hook is not a chain.

**Row 1.** Single crochet in the second chain from the hook, then in each chain across. You skipped one chain, so 16 − 1 = **15 single crochet**.

**Later rows.** Chain 1 and turn. For this exercise that chain is height only. It does not count. Single crochet in the first stitch and in every stitch after it, including the last one. The row is 15 again.

Stop when four rows in a row are 15. Write the counts down: 15, 15, 15, 15. A 14 means an edge stitch was missed. A 16 means the turning chain was used as a stitch, or the hook went into one stitch twice. The [turning chain page](/crochet-turning-chain/) shows that wedge. A [stitch counter](/crochet-stitch-counter/) can hold the number while you look.

When you stop, cut the yarn, pull the tail through the last loop, and weave it through several stitches on the back. Do not cut it flush against the knot. Weaving is covered in [weaving in ends](/weaving-in-ends-and-blocking/).

## 3. Half double crochet (`hdc`)

Between single and double in height. Softer than single crochet, still fairly dense. Useful for hats and blankets.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/half-double-crochet.svg" alt="Diagram of half double crochet: yarn over before inserting, three loops on the hook, then one pull through all three." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Half double crochet starts with a yarn over. The closing pull goes through all three loops.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Yarn over, then insert the hook into the next stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop. Three loops on the hook.
3. Yarn over and pull through all three loops at once.

You yarn over *before* entering the stitch. That extra wrap is the height. Into a chain, start in the **third chain from the hook**. On the next row, chain 2 to turn. Whether that chain counts as a stitch is the usual reason an edge slants, and it is covered on the [turning chain](/crochet-turning-chain/) page. Pick one rule and keep it for the whole swatch.

## 4. Double crochet (`dc`)

![Close-up of a double crochet stitch in progress](../../assets/articles/basic-crochet-stitches-3.jpg "Illustration. Double crochet — yarn over first, then work the loops off in pairs.")

The most-used stitch: tall, quick, and slightly open.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/double-crochet.svg" alt="Diagram of double crochet: three loops on the hook, yarn over and pull through two, then yarn over and pull through the last two." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Double crochet closes two loops at a time. It does not pull through all the loops at once.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Yarn over, then insert the hook into the next stitch.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop. Three loops on the hook.
3. Yarn over and pull through the **first two loops only**. Two loops remain.
4. Yarn over and pull through the last two loops.

You close the loops in two stages, not all at once. Into a chain, start in the **fourth chain from the hook**. The turning chain for the next row is usually 3. The [turning chain](/crochet-turning-chain/) page is where to decide whether that chain replaces the first double crochet.

## 5. Treble crochet (`tr`)

Also written triple crochet. Tall and lacy, for shawls, open patterns, and fabric you want finished fast.

1. Yarn over **twice**, then insert the hook.
2. Yarn over and pull up a loop. Four loops on the hook.
3. Yarn over, pull through the first two loops. Three left.
4. Yarn over, pull through the next two loops. Two left.
5. Yarn over, pull through the last two loops.

Close two loops at a time until one remains. Into a chain, start in the **fifth chain from the hook**. Wrap three times for a double treble, four for a triple treble, then close two loops at a time.

## The pattern behind them

| Stitch | Wraps before inserting | Chain to start in | Turning chain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `sl st` | 0 | — | — |
| `sc` | 0 | 2nd | 1 |
| `hdc` | 1 | 3rd | 2 |
| `dc` | 1 | 4th | 3 |
| `tr` | 2 | 5th | 4 |

Swatch each stitch about 15 wide and 10 rows tall, then make a dishcloth in single crochet or a scarf in half double. A row is successful when the count matches the row before it. Ten single crochets, a chain-1 that you do not count, and ten single crochets on the way back is a straight edge. Eleven means the turning chain was used as an extra stitch.

## The mistakes everyone makes

**Chaining too tightly.** The foundation should be looser than the stitches. If row one is a fight, chain with a hook one size larger, then switch back.

**Gaining or losing stitches at the ends.** Nearly always the turning chain. The [full explanation](/crochet-turning-chain/) is its own page.

**The wrong part of the stitch.** Unless the pattern says otherwise, go under **both** top loops. Front loop only and back loop only are variations a pattern will name.

<figure class="diagram">
<img src="/diagrams/front-back-loops.svg" alt="Diagram comparing a hook under both loops, under the front loop only, and under the back loop only." width="640" height="420" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<figcaption>Diagram. Front loop is the one nearer you. Back loop is the far one. Ordinary stitches use both.</figcaption>
</figure>

**A hard grip on the yarn.** Tight, uneven stitches usually mean the yarn hand is too tense. Let it run more freely.

## Frequently asked

### Which stitch first?

Chain, then single crochet. Slip stitch is easier, and you cannot make a fabric with it.

### Why does my rectangle have holes?

Double crochet and taller stitches are open. That is the stitch. For solid fabric, use single crochet or half double, or drop a hook size.

### Do I need UK terms?

Only for British or Australian patterns. The same abbreviation is a different stitch in each system. The [abbreviations chart](/crochet-abbreviations-chart/) has the conversion.
