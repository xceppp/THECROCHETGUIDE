Working in the round means crocheting in a continuous circle instead of back and forth. Hats, bags, toys, granny squares, doilies, and sleeves are all made this way.

There are two methods, they produce visibly different fabric, and patterns do not always explain which one they mean. Knowing the difference solves most of the problems people have with circular projects.

## The one rule that applies to both

**You do not turn your work.** Unlike rows, where you flip at the end of each one, in the round the right side faces you the entire time and you keep travelling in the same direction. Every round is a right-side round.

If a circular pattern wants you to turn, it will say so explicitly — some do, for texture — but the default is: never turn.

## Method 1: joined rounds

You finish each round, join it to its own beginning with a slip stitch, chain up to the height of the next stitch, and start the next round.

> Work all the stitches of the round, slip stitch into the first stitch of the round, chain up, begin the next round.

**What it looks like:** a visible vertical seam line running up the piece where all the joins stack on top of each other.

**Use it for** granny squares and motifs, anything with a color change every round, striped hats, and anything where you need a clearly defined end of round — lace especially, where the pattern repeat has to line up exactly.

**The downside** is that seam. It is not a mistake and on many projects it looks perfectly fine, but on a plain single-color hat it can be distracting.

### Reducing the jog

The join creates a small stair-step where the round begins slightly higher than it ended. Two standard fixes:

**The invisible join.** Instead of a slip stitch, cut the yarn, thread it through the first stitch with a needle, and pull it snug so the round closes with a stitch-shaped loop rather than a knot. Mostly used on a final round.

**Standing stitches.** Instead of chaining up, start the round with a standing stitch — make a slip knot on the hook and work the first stitch directly. Removes the chain entirely, so there is no chain-shaped gap. Very useful when each round is a different color.

## Method 2: spirals

You simply do not join. When you reach the end of a round, you carry straight on into the next one, working into the first stitch as though the round never ended.

**What it looks like:** completely smooth, no seam, no join line anywhere.

**Use it for** amigurumi and toys, plain single-color hats, socks, and anything tubular where the surface should be unbroken. Nearly all amigurumi patterns are spirals.

**The downside** is that there is nothing marking where a round starts. The fabric gives you no clue.

### You need a stitch marker

This is not optional advice. Put a marker in the first stitch of the round, and each time you come back around to it, move it up into the new first stitch.

A proper split-ring marker is easiest, but a safety pin, a paperclip, a scrap of contrasting yarn, or a bobby pin all work. What does not work is trying to count and remember. Spiral rounds all look identical, and everyone who tries to keep track mentally ends up recounting from the beginning.

**Spirals end with a small step.** Because the last stitch of your final round sits one stitch-height higher than the surrounding fabric, there is a slight bump where you stop. On a toy this is hidden inside a seam. On a hat brim, work one or two slip stitches down into the previous round to taper it, or plan to finish the piece with a joined round.

## Starting the circle

Both methods need a beginning, and there are two ways.

**Magic ring.** An adjustable loop you work into and then pull closed. No hole in the middle. This is the standard for amigurumi and for anything where the center must be solid. [Step-by-step instructions here](/how-to-crochet-a-magic-ring).

**Chain ring.** Chain four or five, slip stitch into the first chain to form a ring, then work into the ring. Simpler, but leaves a small permanent hole. Perfectly correct for doilies, open motifs, and most granny squares — where the hole is part of the design.

## Circle or tube?

The same technique makes both. What decides it is the increases.

**Keep increasing** and the fabric grows outward and stays flat. A flat circle in single crochet adds the same number of stitches every round as the first round had — start with 6 and go 6, 12, 18, 24, 30.

**Stop increasing** and the fabric turns upward and becomes a tube. This is exactly how a hat is made: increase until the flat circle matches the crown diameter, then work even rounds for the depth.

**Too many increases** and the edge ruffles and waves, because there is more fabric than the circumference can hold flat.

So a hat is a flat circle that you stopped increasing, and a toy body is a flat circle you stopped increasing and later started decreasing. There is no separate technique.

## Working into the round below

Unless the pattern says otherwise, insert your hook under **both** top loops of the stitch in the previous round, the same as in flat work.

The common deliberate exception is `BLO` — back loop only — which creates a horizontal ridge. Worked every round in the round, it produces the stretchy ribbed brim you see on hats.

## The problems people actually hit

**"I lost track of where the round starts."** Spiral without a marker. There is no way to find it reliably after the fact; put the marker in and count the current round from the marker forward.

**"My hat has a seam up the side."** You worked joined rounds. Switch to a spiral if you do not want it.

**"My circle is a hexagon."** Your increases are stacking in the same six positions every round instead of being spread out. Stagger them — most patterns handle this for you with instructions like `[sc in next 3, 2 sc in next] 6 times`.

**"My flat circle turned into a bowl."** Too few increases. Fine if you wanted a hat, a problem if you wanted a coaster.

**"There is a gap at the start of every round."** The chain-up in a joined round is thinner than a real stitch. Use a standing stitch instead, or chain one fewer than the stitch height.

## Frequently asked

**Can I switch from spiral to joined mid-project?** Yes. It is common to work a hat body as a spiral and finish the brim with joined rounds so the last round has a clean edge.

**Which should a beginner learn first?** Spiral, with a marker. There is less to remember — no join, no chain-up, no question about whether the chain counts as a stitch.

**Do I count the chain-up as a stitch in joined rounds?** Same unresolved question as in flat work: it depends on the pattern. Check the round's stitch count and be consistent. [More on that here](/crochet-turning-chain).

**Why does my granny square have to be joined?** Because each round starts in a specific corner space and the pattern repeat depends on the rounds lining up. Motif patterns almost always use joined rounds for that reason.
